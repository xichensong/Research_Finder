US-China relations as of 2005 — a period snapshot for backtesting.

This is a deliberately illustrative, non-exhaustive summary of well-known
facts as of 2005, written for the blind-prediction step in backtest.py. It
is not a complete historical record — treat any gap as a genuine gap, not
something to fill in from later knowledge.

Economic:
- China joined the WTO in December 2001 after the US granted Permanent
  Normal Trade Relations (PNTR) in 2000, ending the annual congressional
  fight over China's trade status.
- China's economy has been growing at roughly 9-10% annually for over a
  decade, driven heavily by export manufacturing.
- The US trade deficit with China has been growing rapidly and is a
  recurring subject of political criticism in Washington.
- China's currency (the renminbi) has been pegged to the US dollar; there
  is active US political pressure throughout 2005 for China to let it
  appreciate, with some members of Congress threatening tariff legislation
  over the issue. China moved to a "managed float" against a currency
  basket in July 2005, a small initial revaluation.
- China is not yet widely described as a "technological rival" to the US;
  it is understood primarily as the world's low-cost manufacturing base.

Diplomatic/political:
- The two countries are engaged in the Six-Party Talks (with North Korea,
  South Korea, Japan, and Russia) attempting to address North Korea's
  nuclear program — an area of active US-China cooperation.
- US strategic and military attention is heavily focused on the wars in
  Afghanistan and Iraq.
- Taiwan remains a persistent point of tension; the US maintains its
  "One China" policy while continuing informal ties and arms sales to
  Taiwan under the Taiwan Relations Act. China passed an Anti-Secession
  Law in March 2005 authorizing force if Taiwan formally declares
  independence.
- There is no broad US policy consensus describing China as a primary
  "strategic competitor" the way there would be in later years; China
  policy is debated but not yet the dominant framing of US foreign policy.

Military:
- China's military modernization is proceeding but from a much lower
  technological base than the US; the US is understood to have a
  substantial conventional military advantage in the Asia-Pacific.
- No direct military confrontation between the two countries; occasional
  friction incidents (e.g., the 2001 Hainan Island EP-3 incident) have
  occurred but were resolved diplomatically.

Demographic:
- China's population is roughly 1.3 billion, still several years before
  its "one-child policy" era demographic effects become a major topic of
  discussion; workforce is seen as a long-term economic advantage.
- The US population is roughly 295 million with steady immigration-driven
  growth.

What is NOT yet a major feature of the relationship as of 2005 (for
contrast, so the blind-prediction step doesn't have to guess whether these
existed): no "trade war" tariff conflict, no semiconductor/chip export
control regime aimed at China, no widespread "decoupling" or "de-risking"
policy discourse, no major US public alarm about Chinese AI capability
(the field is nascent), no Belt and Road Initiative (launched 2013).
